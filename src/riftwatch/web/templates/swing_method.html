RiftWatch metric · method

Swing: who moved the game, and by how much

Swing answers one question about a League of Legends game: how much did each player change their team's chance of winning? It's built from a win-probability model trained on {{ '{:,}'.format(m.games) if m else 'stored' }} ranked games, an exact decomposition of each game's result among the ten players, and a set of checks that it measures something real. This page sets out the definitions, the properties that follow from them, the choices that had to be made, and the evidence.

1. The quantity

Let Y = 1 if the blue team wins and 0 if it loses, and let Ft be everything recorded about the game up to time t. The win probability is

Pt = Pr(Y = 1 | Ft).

Before the game P0 reflects only the draft; at the end PT = Y. Over a game, P moves from about 50% to 0 or 100%. Every movement has causes (a kill, a dragon, a lane falling behind), and every cause has players behind it. A player's Swing is the part of that movement they're responsible for, in percentage points. This is the idea behind win probability added in baseball (Mills & Mills, 1970) and its later forms; the work here is making it precise for a game with ten players, continuous economy and simultaneous events.

2. Estimating the win probability

The state. At any second, the game is summarised from the timeline as a vector st seen from one team's side: the minute; gold, XP, CS and level differences; kills; towers; inhibitors currently down; dragons and soul; Baron and Elder taken and whether their buffs are active; heralds, voidgrubs, Atakhan; how many players on each side are alive (from respawn timers){% if m and m.chosen in ('stacked_plus', 'state_plus') %} and how long the dead are still out; which towers are down by tier (outer, inner, base, nexus) and plates taken; how many players on each side have reached levels 6, 11 and 16; gold held but not yet spent; and whether dragon, Elder and Baron are up right now, from spawn and respawn times measured in stored games (dragon at 5:00 and 5:00 after each, Baron at 20:00 and 6:00 after, Elder 6:00 after a soul){% endif %}; each team's finished items by what they give; each team's classes and magic-damage share; and which champions are playing.

Symmetry. A model f(s) gives the chance that the team whose side s is seen from wins. Blue's chance is taken from both sides and averaged:

p̂(st) = ½ [ f(stblue) + 1 − f(stred) ].

So the two teams' chances always add to one, swapping the teams gives 1 − p̂, and a perfectly even state is exactly 50%.

Fitting. The model minimises log loss, −Σ [y log f + (1 − y) log(1 − f)], over every minute of every training game from both sides. Log loss is a proper scoring rule: its expectation is minimised only by the true conditional probability (Gneiting & Raftery, 2007). A model fitted this way estimates Pt itself, rather than just ranking games, which is what a decomposition of probabilities needs.

The model used. A neural network g (two hidden layers, {{ m.network.layers|join(' and ') if m else '64 and 32' }} units{% if m and m.chosen in ('stacked_plus', 'state_plus') %}; five of them trained from different random starts and averaged, which smooths the chances Swing is built from{% endif %}) reads the game state. A regularised logistic layer adds what items, composition and champions contribute:

f(s) = σ( β0 · logit g(sstate) + βT z(s) ),

where z holds item differences, item-against-team matchups (armor × the enemy's physical share, and so on), class differences and their change with the minute, and each champion's own and minute-scaled terms, with an L2 penalty on β (the champion terms penalised hardest). A network fed those inputs directly memorises games by their line-up (they don't change during a game), which a linear layer can't do. Platt scaling, c(p) = σ(a logit p + b), calibrates it on separate games. Unlike isotonic calibration it's smooth, so small changes in the state can't fall on a flat step and count for nothing.

Honest evaluation. Games, not moments, are split 70 / 15 / 15 into training, calibration and test, so no minute of a test game is ever seen in training. Networks are stopped on held-out training games, and the version used is chosen on games none of the versions trained on.

3. The decomposition

Take the moments 0 = τ0 < τ1 < … < τK = T: every whole minute, plus every kill, objective, structure and finished item. At an event e, the state just before it and just after it (the same instant, with the event counted, and a kill's bounty added) give a jump

Δe = p̂(se+) − p̂(se−).

Within each minute, whatever moved that no event explains is the drift: gold, XP and pressure built up between fights,

rm = [ p̂m+1 − p̂m ] − Σe in minute m Δe.

Because a telescoping sum cancels everything in between, the game's result splits exactly into

 Y − ½ = (p̂0 − ½) + Σe Δe + Σm rm + (Y − p̂T)   (1)

draft + events + drift + the last step to the result. Each term goes to blue with its sign and to red with the opposite sign, and is shared among a team's five players by weights wi ≥ 0 with Σ wi = 1 (section 4). Swing is reported in percentage points (×100).

Proposition 1 (exact and zero-sum). In every game, the winning team's Swing adds up to +50 and the losing team's to −50.

Proof. By (1) the terms add up to Y − ½ = ±½ for blue, and each is shared out in full because the weights add to one. Red receives the negative of every term. ∎ (Checked to 10−9 in the test suite, under every set of sharing rules.)

Proposition 2 (only the unexpected counts). If p̂ = P, every term after the draft has expected value zero given what was known before it.

Proof. Pt = E[Y | Ft] is a martingale: for t′ > t, E[Pt′ | Ft] = E[ E[Y | Ft′] | Ft ] = Pt, by the law of iterated expectations. So each increment of (1) averages zero given the past. ∎

In practice: a team that was expected to win gets no credit for winning as expected, only for doing better than expected. Steady farming that the model already prices in earns nothing; outfarming what was expected does. The draft term p̂0 − ½ is the one expected part, and it's reported on its own.

Proposition 3 (why a moment moved the chance). Let an event change the groups of inputs G = {g1, …, gk}: a player down, the bounty gold, the kill score, the dragon, an item's armor. Let v(S) be the model's chance with only the groups in S changed. The split

φg = ΣS ⊆ G ∖ {g} |S|! (k − |S| − 1)! / k! · [ v(S ∪ {g}) − v(S) ]

is the only one that is efficient (the parts add up to Δe), symmetric (changes that act alike get the same), gives nothing to a change that never matters, and is additive (Shapley, 1953). With k ≤ 6, every one of the 2k states is put to the model, so the split is exact, not sampled.

4. Sharing: the rules

Section 3 is fixed by the model. How a term is shared among five teammates is not: the timeline doesn't say who made a dragon possible. These rules are the only choices in Swing that aren't learned from data. Where the data measures a contribution, the rule uses it; where it doesn't, an equal share stands in for what can't be seen.

4a. Farm given up for a play

A laner who roams for a kill, or a jungler who ganks, stops farming. The drift terms charge that later, as minutes in which they out-earned their lane opponent by less, so Swing already holds the cost but puts it down to "economy" rather than the play. RiftWatch measures the cost and moves it onto the play.

Farming minutes. A minute m is a farming minute for player i when they're alive throughout, in their own area at both minute frames and take part in no kill, death, objective or structure. Until 14:00, when turret plates fall, the area is their lane (for a jungler the jungle and river). After that it's anywhere but a base. Minutes in their own base (a back) are neither farming nor away.

Pace. With Fi their farming minutes this game, xi,m the CS they took in minute m and μr,m the typical CS in a farming minute m for role r (measured over stored games), their pace is πi = (ΣFi xi,m + k) / (ΣFi μr,m + k). Here k is three minutes of typical farm, which pulls a short sample towards 1.

CS given up. For a run W of minutes away holding plays (at most two minutes either side of them), plus up to two farming minutes after it (farm caught up), the CS given up is Δ = max(0, ΣW (πiμr,m − xi,m)). It's turned into gold and XP with what a CS is worth for that role at that stage of the game in stored farming minutes. Gold uses the slope of gold on CS, because passive gold keeps coming while you're away. XP uses XP per CS, because lane XP stops when you leave.

Its worth, and what's charged. Let v(Δ) = p̂(send with Δ more CS, and its gold and XP, for the player's team and lane) − p̂(send), from the same model at the end of the window. The farm was worth v(Δ). But the chance already moved at the play by what such plays usually lead to, including their usual farm cost: a kill or a death is priced at its average consequences, so charging all of v(Δ) would count that part twice. The charge is c = v(Δ) − v(Δ̄), where Δ̄ is the CS given up by windows of the same role and stage of the game, with or without the player dying in them, in stored games, averaged with each window weighted by what a CS was worth at its end. Since c ≈ (worth of a CS) × (Δ − Δ̄), that weighting makes the charge average zero over stored games, as an expected cost should. A roam that cost more waves than usual is charged; one that cost fewer (the wave shoved first) is credited. Plays in one window share it equally. Supports aren't measured, since farming isn't their job.

Because c is taken off the play and added back to the same player's economy, the sums in section 3 are untouched and every team still adds up to exactly ±50. A roam is judged net of the waves it cost, and the economy part isn't blamed for it. The profile's "Were your plays worth the farm?" table adds these up by kind of play against the same for your role.

5. The Swing rating

One game's Swing is noisy. A player's level is estimated from many, under a simple model. Let xij be player i's Swing in game j minus the average for their role:

xij = θi + εij,   Var(θi) = τ2,   Var(εij) = σ2,   ε independent across games.

The best linear estimate of θi from n games with mean x̄i, and its uncertainty, are (Efron & Morris, 1975)

θ̂i = x̄i · nτ2 / (nτ2 + σ2),    sd = √( τ2σ2 / (nτ2 + σ2) ).

Both variances come from the data, and the estimators are checked in the test suite on simulated players with known true levels, where they recover the true spread and give pure noise a reliability near zero. Here τ2 is the covariance between players' averages over two halves of their games: the noise in disjoint games is independent, so only the true level is shared. σ2 is the average spread of a player's own games. {% if v and v.rating %}Here τ = {{ v.rating.tau }} points and σ = {{ v.rating.sigma }}, so a rating is pulled towards the role average as if {{ v.rating.k }} average games were added: 20 games move it {{ (100 * 20 / (20 + v.rating.k))|round|int }}% of the way to the player's own average.{% endif %}

A statistic about players should do three things: repeat (a player's level should show in any half of their games), predict (it should say something about results to come), and not hinge on arbitrary choices. 7. What it's for

  • Reading a game. Swing turns a game into its turning points: which moments moved the chance most, and (Proposition 3) why. That one death cost 4.5 points because a player was down for 39 seconds with Baron up and a shutdown handed over, not because deaths are bad in general.
  • Coaching. Deaths are the most repeatable part of Swing for a player, and the profile splits them into habits (an objective lost before respawning, caught by three, dying with a back's gold) measured against everyone in the same role. That points at the habit costing the most.
  • Judging a player. The rating (section 5) says how much win chance a player adds per game against their role, with a margin, pulled towards average until enough games say otherwise.

8. Limits

  • Value, not causation. The model says what states like this have led to, not what would have happened otherwise. A kill's worth is what kills like it have been followed by.
  • What the timeline sees. Positions come once a minute; vision, crowd control, peel and threat that changes the enemy's plans show up only through their results (a kill, a lead that grows). The equal shares in section 4 are where that unseen work is credited, roughly.
  • Team terms. The draft, half of every objective, the economy's even half and the last step are shared evenly. They carry little about any one player, and the validation shows it.
  • Like win probability added in baseball, a "what happened" stat. The same kill is worth more at 35 minutes with Baron up than at 6. That is the point of Swing, but it means one game says little about skill on its own. The rating, with its margin, is the skill estimate.
